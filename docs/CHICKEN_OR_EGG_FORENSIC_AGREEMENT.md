### 1. Purpose and governing principle

This document establishes an evidence-led way to describe the relationship between creative concepts and technical implementations, preserve provenance, credit requested contributors, and support reproducibility.

The Chicken-or-Egg Principle: a concept can lead to an implementation; an implementation can reveal or reshape a concept; and both can develop in a feedback loop. No party may claim exclusive origin merely because their contribution appears first in the surviving record. Describe the history supported by evidence rather than forcing a single origin story.

### 4. Separate concept and implementation evidence

For each material feature, maintain linked but separate records.

Concept record:
- What was proposed, by whom if known, and when?
- What is the earliest available dated evidence?
- Was it a sketch, conversation, prompt, design document, prototype, or other artifact?
- What uncertainties remain?

Implementation record:
- What code, prompt, configuration, or artifact implements it?
- Who committed or authored it, if established?
- Which repository, path, commit, and test demonstrate it?
- Does the implementation match, reinterpret, or depart from the concept?

Relationship record:
- Explain whether implementation realizes, changes, challenges, or inspires the concept.
- Keep chronology and interpretation distinct.
- A commit timestamp proves a repository event, not necessarily first conception or sole authorship.
- A recollection is useful testimony but should be labeled User-reported unless corroborated.

### 5. Evidence and chain of custody

For forensic work:
1. Preserve original files and archives without modification.
2. Use working copies for extraction, conversion, and analysis.
3. Record filename, byte size, SHA-256 hash, source URL/location, retrieval time, and tool/version where available.
4. Record archive members, skipped entries, errors, and path-safety checks.
5. Record repository commit IDs and exact file paths created from each source.
6. Record test commands, environment, outputs, and limitations.

Never describe an archive as extracted unless it has actually been unpacked and inventoried. Never infer authorship from a filename, directory name, or archive timestamp alone. Distinguish source, derived, and generated materials.

Label claims:
- Verified: directly supported by inspectable evidence.
- Corroborated: supported by multiple independent sources.
- User-reported: stated by a contributor but not independently verified.
- Inferred: reasoned interpretation, not direct record.
- Proposed: future design or requested attribution.
- Unknown/disputed: evidence is absent, incomplete, or contested.

### 6. Credit-forward rule

When an artifact is reused, redistributed, quoted, or developed further:
- Preserve existing copyright, license, and attribution notices.
- Include requested contributor names in project acknowledgments where appropriate and where consent and roles are confirmed.
- Credit specific contributions only when supported by records or confirmed by the contributor.
- Do not erase earlier contributors when adding new ones.
- Do not convert a courtesy acknowledgment into a claim of legal ownership.
- Do not imply that all named people approved a release when approval is not recorded.

### 9. Chicken-or-Egg dispute procedure

If contributors disagree about origin:
1. Preserve all versions; do not rewrite history.
2. List each claim neutrally.
3. Attach earliest available evidence for each claim.
4. Separate concept chronology from implementation chronology.
5. Identify missing records and competing interpretations.
6. Use terms such as co-developed, independently proposed, implemented from, or later refined only where supported.
7. If unresolved, record the matter as disputed and credit confirmed contributions without unsupported priority claims.

No automated model may decide authorship, legal ownership, or consent.
